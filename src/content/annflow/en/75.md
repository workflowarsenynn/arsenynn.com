---
title: "Keyboard layers"
---

The most basic thing, without which I can hardly find my way around the system any more, is **keyboard layers**.

I went through different approaches but settled on this pairing:

**Karabiner + Hammerspoon**

**Karabiner-Elements** makes the "input hardware" predictable:

it decides which physical key turns into what (tap/hold, a hyper modifier, virtual F keys, rules per layout).

**Hammerspoon** turns these signals into actions:

**launching and focusing apps**, managing **windows** (moving, layouts, moving between monitors, restoring sizes), **window navigation**, **media control** for specific apps, **system things** and quick commands usually hidden in menus. And most importantly, these aren't just bindings but **logic and context** (state checks, focusing the right window before an action, delays, fallbacks, scenarios). Hammerspoon also works as a **local server** for me: I bring up a small HTTP endpoint and can trigger my commands remotely (for example, from Shortcuts on other devices). All in Lua, no magic and no UI mazes.

**Why I chose Karabiner + Hammerspoon.**

I tried various ready-made solutions, but they almost always have the same problem: I get exactly the set of features the author came up with. That's fine as long as your scenario matches the "average user". But as soon as layers, context, non-standard rules, reliable fallbacks and your own logic for windows and media come in, you run into the limits of the interface and the philosophy of a particular program.

Here the approach is different:

- **Karabiner** gives me control at the input level: I can turn any physical keys into clean signals and build layers so that typing doesn't break and conflicts don't occur.
- **Hammerspoon** gives me control at the behaviour level: I describe actions in code, add checks, context, delays and fallbacks, and get a result that works the way I need, not "the way the UI intended".

In the end it's not "yet another program with settings" but a platform where I set the rules myself. It's for this flexibility that I chose this approach.
